I just found out

You may have more questions than answers.

Discovery can make the past feel suddenly unstable. You may be trying to understand what happened, what your wife knew or felt at different points, whether the relationship was really consensual, and what any of it means for your marriage. You do not have to resolve those questions all at once.

Was this really abuse if she was an adult?

Start by learning about pastoral authority, entrusted care, grooming, professional boundaries, and the power imbalance inherent in clergy-congregant relationships.

Why didn’t she stop it or tell me sooner?

Grooming, shame, fear, dependency, spiritual confusion, trauma, and concern about consequences can all complicate resistance and disclosure. Her behavior may make more sense when viewed in that context.

Did she love him? Did she choose him?

Survivors can have attachment, affection, longing, confusion, or even protectiveness toward a person who exploited them. Those feelings do not settle the question of whether the clergy member misused entrusted authority.

What am I allowed to feel?

Anger, grief, betrayal, empathy, resentment, protectiveness, sadness, and love can coexist. Your wife's abuse and your secondary trauma are different experiences, but both deserve care.

What does this mean for our marriage?

You do not need to make immediate decisions about trust, intimacy, reconciliation, separation, church, or forgiveness. Major decisions are often clearer when both partners have safe support and more information.

Where do I put my own pain?

Your wife should not have to carry all of your reactions to what happened to her. A trauma-informed therapist, trusted support person, and other husbands who understand ACSA can give your experience somewhere safe to go.

For the first days and weeks: seek understanding before certainty. Learn about ACSA, protect your wife's agency and privacy, avoid repeated interrogation, find support of your own, and resist pressure—from yourself or others—to decide immediately what the future of the marriage or your faith must look like.
Understand the dynamics

Why this doesn't fit the “affair” framework

The questions that come after discovery often become clearer when you understand clergy power, entrusted care, grooming, secrecy, spiritual authority, and the ways trauma can shape attachment and disclosure.

It is about power, not simply sex

Clergy can carry unusual spiritual, emotional, social, and institutional authority. When a person entrusted with pastoral care sexualizes that relationship, the power differential changes how consent and responsibility should be understood.

Grooming can create confusion

Boundary violations may develop gradually through special attention, emotional intimacy, counseling, secrecy, spiritual language, dependency, or escalating physical contact. Your wife may have struggled to understand what was happening even while it was happening.

The leader owns the boundary

A clergy member occupies the position of entrusted authority. Ethical responsibility for maintaining appropriate pastoral boundaries rests with the leader—not with the person who came to that leader for ministry, guidance, counseling, or care.

A difficult but important distinction: understanding your wife as someone who was exploited does not require you to deny your own feelings of betrayal, loss, anger, or confusion. It does ask you to avoid making her responsible for the clergy member's misuse of authority.
ACSA by the numbers

This is not as rare—or as simple—as many families are led to believe.

Research on adult clergy sexual misconduct remains limited, but several studies provide important context. These figures are not meant to reduce individual stories to statistics; they show why ACSA deserves to be recognized as a distinct form of abuse and why institutional response matters.

3.1%

of women attending religious services at least monthly reported a sexual advance from a religious leader in their own congregation since age 18 in a national U.S. study—roughly 1 in 33 in that population.

92%

of the reported clergy sexual advances in that study occurred in secret, underscoring how secrecy can shape both the abuse and what spouses later discover.

67%

of the religious leaders making those advances were married to someone else, according to the same national study.

<10%

of 159 female ACSA survivors in a later national study said their congregation provided help and support after they reported; about half reported being blamed and about half ignored.

Sources: Chaves & Garland, Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion (2009); Pooler & Barros-Lane, national study of adult women sexually abused by clergy (2022). The 3.1% figure applies to women who attended religious services at least monthly and should not be generalized to all women.
