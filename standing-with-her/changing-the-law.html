Changing the law

States are beginning to name clergy sexual exploitation in law.

The legal landscape remains a patchwork. Some states directly address clergy sexual conduct within relationships of pastoral counseling or spiritual authority; others use broader abuse-of-trust, counselor, or exploitation statutes.

Why the law matters

These laws reflect a growing recognition that the relevant question is often not simply whether an adult said “yes,” but whether spiritual authority was exploited.

Changing the law

States are increasingly confronting clergy sexual exploitation of adults.

There is no single national ACSA criminal statute, and state laws vary widely. Some states expressly address sexual conduct by clergy in relationships of spiritual authority or pastoral counseling. Others reach clergy through laws governing counselors, psychotherapists, fiduciary relationships, coercion, or exploitation of emotional dependency.

State by state

ACSA protections are a patchwork. Whether particular conduct is criminal depends on the jurisdiction, the clergy-survivor relationship, and the wording of the statute.

2026

Georgia enacted a new offense addressing improper sexual contact by a clergy member in a pastoral-counseling or spiritual-authority relationship.

15 years

Georgia's 2026 law provides a 15-year limitations period for prosecutions under its new clergy sexual-contact provision.

Active bills

Other states continue considering changes. New York's 2025–26 S9995, for example, remained in the Senate Codes Committee as of October 2026.

What these laws are trying to recognize

Traditional sexual-offense law often focuses on force, incapacity, or an explicit lack of consent. ACSA laws can instead focus on the relationship: whether a clergy member used pastoral counseling, spiritual authority, entrusted care, emotional dependency, coercion, deception, or a professional role to obtain sexual access.

That distinction matters because the central question in many ACSA cases is not simply whether an adult said “yes,” but whether a trusted spiritual authority exploited the power, access, vulnerability, and dependency created by the pastoral relationship.

Examples of different approaches

  • Georgia: its 2026 law creates first- and second-degree offenses involving clergy sexual contact while in a pastoral-counseling or spiritual-authority relationship when specified forms of undue influence, coercion, deception, intimidation, or related conduct are used.
  • New York: S9995 would amend consent rules for sexual conduct involving clergy or ministers providing pastoral counseling; as of October 2026 it is an active bill in committee, not enacted law.
  • Other states: existing statutes differ considerably. Some focus on clergy specifically; others treat clergy as counselors or psychotherapists, or focus on abuse of trust or emotional dependency.
Why husbands may care about this: learning that legislatures are treating clergy sexual exploitation as a distinct abuse-of-power problem can help explain why the “affair” framework is often inadequate. But a statute's existence—or absence—does not by itself determine what your wife experienced psychologically, ethically, clinically, or spiritually.
