Your marriage

Two people can be wounded in different ways.

Your wife is the primary survivor. You may also be carrying betrayal, grief, anger, spiritual disorientation, and changes in trust and intimacy. This page is about holding both realities without making them compete.

A both/and reality

Supporting her does not require denying your own pain. Caring for yourself does not require minimizing what happened to her.

Two people, two experiences

You may be supporting her while hurting yourself

Secondary-survivor spouses can occupy an unusually difficult dual role: a central support for the primary survivor while simultaneously experiencing consequences of the abuse and, sometimes, the institution's response.

What she may be carrying

  • Confusion about how the relationship became sexualized
  • Shame or self-blame, even when responsibility belonged with the leader
  • Trauma responses and difficulty trusting herself or others
  • Loss of church, community, friendships, vocation, or spiritual identity
  • Fear of not being believed—or of being labeled as having had an “affair”
  • Complicated feelings toward the person who harmed her

What you may be carrying

  • Anger toward the clergy member and institution
  • Grief for what changed in your marriage and family
  • Questions about trust, intimacy, sexuality, and what you knew
  • Powerlessness because you could not protect someone you love
  • Spiritual betrayal and loss of a faith community you trusted
  • Isolation because few people understand the complexity

Your job is not to become her investigator, therapist, judge, or savior. Your job is to become a safer partner while finding safe places to carry your own pain.

Ways to support her

Believe before interrogating.
Questions can be necessary, but repeated cross-examination can deepen shame and defensiveness.
Learn about power and grooming.
A framework for exploitation can make confusing behavior more comprehensible without erasing complexity.
Give her agency back.
Avoid controlling disclosure, reporting, confrontation, therapy, faith, or forgiveness in an attempt to repair what happened.
Let intimacy move at a safe pace.
Sexual and emotional closeness may be affected. Neither spouse benefits from treating recovery as a deadline.

Ways to care for yourself

Find your own witnesses.
You need people with whom you can speak candidly without making your wife carry all of your reactions.
Allow complicated emotions.
Love, anger, empathy, grief, resentment, protectiveness, and confusion can coexist.
Consider specialized therapy.
A trauma-informed clinician familiar with clergy abuse, betrayal, and couples dynamics may be more helpful than generic marriage advice.
Resist forced timelines.
Recovery is rarely linear—for her, for you, or for the relationship.
